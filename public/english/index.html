Word of the day: deadline

Lesson 0222 Nov 2026

How to say it: DED-line (IPA: /ˈded.laɪn/)

Meaning: a time or date by which something must be finished.

Examples:

  • The deadline for the application is Friday at 5 p.m.
  • I need to finish this report before the deadline.
  • We asked for a later deadline, because the files arrived late.

Useful phrases:

  • meet a deadline — finish on time
  • miss a deadline — finish late, or not finish
  • a tight deadline — you don't have much time
  • extend a deadline — give more time (the person in charge can do this; you usually have to ask)

Be careful: a deadline is the last moment, not the moment you should start. If the deadline is Friday, starting on Friday is usually too late.

Practice (one line in the comments, if you want): write one sentence about a deadline this week — a real one, or an invented one. For example: “My deadline for ___ is ___.”

Word of the day On Telegram

Word of the day: feedback

Lesson 0323 Nov 2026

How to say it: FEED-back (IPA: /ˈfiːd.bæk/)

Meaning: comments about your work, given so you can improve it. In everyday English, this is an uncountable noun: we usually say feedback, not “a feedback”. We can say “a piece of feedback” or “some feedback” when we need a countable phrase.

Examples:

  • My teacher gave me useful feedback on my essay.
  • Could I get some feedback on this email before I send it?
  • The feedback from the client was mostly positive.

Useful phrases:

  • give / offer feedback
  • get / receive feedback
  • positive feedback — what went well
  • constructive feedback — what to improve, said in a useful way
  • feedback on (something) — not usually “feedback about” in careful work writing, though you will hear both

Be careful: “I want a feedback” is a common slip. Prefer “I want some feedback” or “I would like feedback on this draft”.

Practice: write one polite sentence asking for feedback on something you made. For example: “Could I have some feedback on ___?”

Common mistake

Common mistake: since vs for

Lesson 04From the first series

🧩 Common mistake: since and for (time).

Both can talk about how long something has been true. The difference is what follows them.

Use for with a duration — a length of time:

  • I have lived here for three years.
  • She has been on the call for 20 minutes.
  • We have studied this chapter for a week.

Use since with a starting point — a time, a date, or an event:

  • I have lived here since 2023.
  • She has been on the call since 2 p.m.
  • We have studied this chapter since Monday.

A quick test: if you can ask “How long?” the answer often uses for. If you can ask “Since when?” the answer uses since.

Notice the verb: we usually use a present perfect form (have lived, has been, have studied) for a situation that started in the past and is still true now. “I live here since 2023” is a common slip. Prefer “I have lived here since 2023.”

More examples:

  • Correct: I have known him for a long time. / I have known him since school.
  • Incorrect: I have known him since a long time.
  • Correct: The office has been closed since the holiday.
  • Incorrect: The office has been closed for the holiday started.

Practice: finish these two sentences about your own life (they can be simple):

  • I have … for …
  • I have … since …

Quiz: deadline, feedback, since / for

Lesson 05From the first series

✅ Quick quiz. Three questions, one answer each. The poll below is the first question; questions 2 and 3 follow as separate polls. Take them in order. Explanations are in the poll (and here, if you are reading later).

Q1. “The _____ for this assignment is Thursday.”
What fits best?

  • deadline
  • feedback
  • meeting
  • since

Correct: deadline. A deadline is the last date or time for the work.

Q2. Which sentence is correct?

  • I want a feedback on my draft.
  • I want some feedback on my draft.
  • I want some feedbacks on my draft.

Correct: “I want some feedback on my draft.” Feedback is usually uncountable. Prefer some feedback, or a piece of feedback, not “a feedback”.

Q3. “I have worked here _____ 2021.”

  • for
  • since
  • during
  • until

Correct: since. 2021 is a starting point, not a duration. “For” would need a length of time: “for four years”.

Word of the day On Telegram

Word of the day: reliable

Lesson 0623 Nov 2026

How to say it: ri-LY-uh-buhl (IPA: /rɪˈlaɪ.ə.bəl/)

Meaning: if a person or a thing is reliable, you can trust them to do what they should do, again and again.

Examples:

  • She's a reliable colleague — if she says she will send the file, she sends it.
  • I need a reliable internet connection for the exam.
  • This is not a reliable source; we should check another one.

Related words:

  • reliably (adverb): The app works reliably on my phone.
  • reliability (noun): We're not sure about the reliability of that number.
  • unreliable (opposite): The bus is unreliable in the rain.

Useful phrases:

  • a reliable source — one you can reasonably trust (still check important facts)
  • a reliable estimate — a careful guess, not a wild one
  • dependable is a close synonym for people; reliable is the more usual word for things and for sources

Practice: write one sentence about a reliable person, tool, or source in your week. For example: “___ is reliable because ___.”

Phrasal verb

Phrasal verbs: look up and look into

Lesson 07From the first series

🧩 Phrasal verbs of the day: look up and look into.

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word (up, into, after…) that changes the meaning. You often cannot guess the meaning from the two parts alone.

look up (something) — search for information in a list, a dictionary, or online.

  • If you don't know the word, look it up.
  • I looked up the opening hours on the official website.
  • Look up the verb in a dictionary, don't guess the preposition.

look into (something) — investigate, or try to find out more about a problem or a request. It is more than a quick search.

  • The team is looking into the bug in the form.
  • I'll look into cheaper options and email you tomorrow.
  • The university is looking into the complaint.

Quick contrast:

  • You look up a word, a number, a time, a spelling.
  • You look into a problem, a complaint, a possibility, a delay.

Word order: look up is often separable when the object is a pronoun: “look it up”, not “look up it”. look into is not used that way: “look into it”, not “look it into”.

Not today, but nearby: look after means “take care of” (look after a child, look after a guest). Different meaning again — we'll come back to it another day, so the three don't get mixed.

Practice: write two short sentences, one with look up and one with look into, about your work or your studies.

Quiz

Quiz: look up, look into, reliable

Lesson 08From the first series

✅ Quick quiz. Two questions tonight, as separate polls.

Q1. “I don't know this word. I'll _____ in the dictionary.”

  • look it into
  • look it up
  • look into it
  • look up it

Correct: look it up. You search for a word. With a pronoun, the word order is look it up, not look up it.

Q2. “The manager said she would _____ the delay and get back to us.”

  • look up
  • look it up
  • look into
  • look after

Correct: look into. A delay is a problem to investigate, not a word to search for. “Look into the delay” or “look into it”.

Word of the day On Telegram

Word of the day: postpone

Lesson 0924 Nov 2026

How to say it: post-PONE (IPA: /pəʊstˈpəʊn/ in many British pronunciations, /poʊstˈpoʊn/ in many American ones). Stress on the second part: pone.

Meaning: to move something to a later time or date. The event is still going to happen; it is not cancelled.

Examples:

  • They postponed the meeting until Thursday.
  • The exam was postponed because of the storm.
  • Can we postpone this until I have the figures?

Close words, not the same:

  • cancel — it will not happen (The flight was cancelled.)
  • delay — it happens later than planned, often by a shorter, sometimes unplanned amount of time (The train is delayed by 20 minutes.)
  • put off — a more informal phrasal verb with a similar meaning to postpone (Don't put it off until the last minute.)

Useful patterns:

  • postpone something until + a day or a date
  • postpone something for + a duration (postpone the launch for a week)
  • be postponed (often used when you are not the person who decided)

Practice: write one sentence about something that was postponed, or that you would like to postpone. Say until when, if you can.

📌 This afternoon: make and do — a pair of verbs that doesn't follow one simple rule, but a useful short list will get you through most work and study sentences.

Common mistake

Common mistake: make vs do

Lesson 10From the first series

🧩 Common mistake: make and do.

There isn't one perfect rule that covers every phrase, but there is a useful pattern, and then a short list to learn.

A helpful pattern:

  • make often means “create, produce, or cause a result” — you can often see a result after it.
  • do often means “perform a task or an activity”, including work, chores, and general “tasks”.

Common work and study phrases with make:

  • make a decision, make a plan, make a list
  • make a mistake, make an effort, make progress
  • make a call, make an appointment
  • make a suggestion, make a comment
  • make money (earn it); make a difference

Common work and study phrases with do:

  • do work, do homework, do a job, do business
  • do an exercise, do a quiz, do a test / an exam (in some varieties you also take a test / an exam)
  • do research (research is usually uncountable here)
  • do someone a favour
  • do your best

Examples:

  • I need to make a decision by Friday. (not “do a decision”)
  • Have you done your homework? (not “made your homework”)
  • She made a useful suggestion in the meeting.
  • He did his best, but he still made a few mistakes.

If you are not sure, look the phrase up (yesterday's phrasal verb) rather than guessing. A short personal list of the 10 phrases you actually need is better than a page of rules.

Practice: choose two phrases from the lists — one with make, one with do — and write a sentence about this week.

Quiz: postpone, make, do

Lesson 11From the first series

✅ Quick quiz. Three short questions, as separate polls.

Q1. “The concert isn't cancelled. It's just _____ until next month.”

  • postponed
  • delayed to cancel
  • postponed away
  • made off

Correct: postponed. The event will still happen, but at a later date.

Q2. “I need to _____ a decision before the deadline.”

  • do
  • make
  • make do
  • did

Correct: make. The usual phrase is make a decision.

Q3. “I have to _____ my homework before I go out.”

  • make
  • do
  • make up
  • postpone on

Correct: do. The usual phrase is do your homework. (Make up homework is not the standard phrase.)

Phrasal verb

Phrasal verb: catch up

Lesson 12From the first series

🧩 Phrasal verb of the morning: catch up.

This one has a few related meanings. The two most useful for work and study are below. The small words after it matter.

  • catch up (with someone / something) — reach the same level or the same point, after being behind.
  • I was ill last week, so I need to catch up with the class.
  • She's trying to catch up on the emails she missed.
  • If you miss a lecture, catch up before the next one if you can.

You'll hear catch up on work, emails, or sleep (the things you do), and catch up with a person, a class, or a group (the people or the level you want to reach). Both are common.

  • catch up (with someone) — talk, so you can hear each other's news after some time.
  • Let's catch up over coffee this weekend.
  • It was good to catch up after so long.

Be careful:

  • catch up with the class = reach their level, or talk to them later, depending on context. If it's study, meaning 1 is the usual one.
  • “I need to catch up the class” (without with / on) is a common slip. Prefer catch up with or catch up on.

Practice: write one sentence with catch up on or catch up with about this week — work, class, or a friend.

📌 This afternoon: borrow and lend — two verbs that are easy to swap if you think in another language first.

Common mistake

Common mistake: borrow vs lend

Lesson 13From the first series

🧩 Common mistake: borrow and lend.

The two verbs look at the same action from different sides. If you mix them up, the sentence can mean the opposite of what you wanted.

borrow — you take something for a short time, and you will give it back.

  • Can I borrow your charger?
  • I borrowed this book from the library.
  • She borrowed some notes from a classmate.

lend — you give something to someone for a short time, and you expect it back.

  • Can you lend me your charger?
  • The library lends books to students.
  • He lent me his notes for the weekend.

A simple test: the person who goes away with the thing is the one who borrows. The person who lets them take it is the one who lends.

Useful patterns:

  • borrow something from someone
  • lend someone something / lend something to someone
  • Past forms: borrow → borrowed; lend → lent (not “lended”).

Examples side by side:
• Please can I borrow your laptop for an hour?
• Please can you lend me your laptop for an hour?
Both are polite requests for the same situation. The subject changes: I borrow, you lend.

Incorrect, if you mean “I want to use your charger for a while”:
• Can you borrow me your charger?
Prefer: Can you lend me your charger? or Can I borrow your charger?

Practice: write two versions of the same request — one with borrow and one with lend — about a real object (a book, a charger, a calculator).
